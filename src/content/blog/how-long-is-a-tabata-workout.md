How long is a tabata workout, strictly speaking: four minutes. Eight rounds of 20 seconds work and 10 seconds rest is 240 seconds, and there is no arithmetic to argue about. The reason the question gets asked at all is that almost nobody stops after one block.

## The four-minute block

| Part           | Count | Each | Total     |
| -------------- | ----- | ---- | --------- |
| Work intervals | 8     | 20s  | 160s      |
| Rest intervals | 8     | 10s  | 80s       |
| **One block**  |       |      | **4 min** |

The eighth rest is usually included in the count even though nothing follows it, which is why the [tabata timer](/tabata) runs to 4:00 flat rather than 3:50. Trim it if you like; the difference is ten seconds and you will want them.

## Why classes run four or five blocks

Four minutes of genuine all-out effort is a real training stimulus, but it is not a class. A studio selling a 30-minute slot has to fill 30 minutes, and a single movement for four minutes does not.

So the standard structure is blocks: one movement per block, a longer rest between blocks, four or five blocks total.

| Blocks | Work   | Rest between | Total work + rest | Plus warm-up and cooldown |
| ------ | ------ | ------------ | ----------------- | ------------------------- |
| 2      | 8 min  | 1 x 1 min    | 9 min             | ~18 min                   |
| 3      | 12 min | 2 x 1 min    | 14 min            | ~24 min                   |
| 4      | 16 min | 3 x 1 min    | 19 min            | ~29 min                   |
| 5      | 20 min | 4 x 1 min    | 24 min            | ~34 min                   |

The between-block rest is the part people cut, and it is the part that decides whether block four is training or theatre. One full minute standing still, breathing, is the minimum. Ninety seconds is better if the movement was a big one like swings or burpees.

Four blocks with a five-minute warm-up and a few minutes of walking afterwards is the honest answer to how long a tabata session takes: about half an hour, of which sixteen minutes involve any work.

## What the original study actually did

The 1996 paper from Izumi Tabata's group at Japan's National Institute of Fitness and Sports in Kanoya is worth being precise about, because the session length is not the interesting number.

Their high-intensity group trained **five days a week for six weeks**. Four of those days were the 20/10 protocol at roughly 170% of VO2max on a cycle ergometer, done to exhaustion or eight rounds, whichever came first. The fifth day was a steady 30-minute ride with a short set of intervals attached. The comparison group rode 60 minutes at 70% VO2max, five days a week.

So the weekly load in the short group was somewhere around 20 minutes of interval work plus that fifth session, not four minutes once. And the intensity was lab-set, not self-selected. If you are doing one four-minute block twice a week at a pace you chose yourself, you are doing something else with the same shape. Still useful, just not the study.

The result that made it famous: the short group improved both aerobic and anaerobic capacity, the one-hour group improved only aerobic. More detail on the protocol is in [what is a tabata timer](/blog/what-is-a-tabata-timer).

## How long it should be for you

- **First time.** One block. Four minutes. Pick something you cannot injure yourself with, like a bike or high knees, and find out what round six feels like before planning a session.
- **Two to four weeks in.** Two or three blocks, different movement each, full minute between. Fifteen to twenty-five minutes including a warm-up.
- **Conditioned.** Four or five blocks, or drop the block structure and run a longer interval set instead. Twenty minutes of 20/10 is a lot of transitions; at some point a 30/30 or a sprint set is a better use of the time.

Longer rounds are the other lever. [30/15 x 8](/tabata/30-15-8) is six minutes and considerably more sustainable, because 15 seconds is closer to an actual breath than 10 is. [20/10 x 4](/tabata/20-10-4) is two minutes and the right size for a finisher or a first attempt.

## Setting it up

For a single block, the [tabata timer](/tabata) defaults are already 20/10 x 8. For multi-block work, the simplest approach is to run the block, then stand there and count your own minute, then run it again. It is less precise than a nested timer and much harder to get wrong mid-workout.

If you want the whole session on one timer including the between-block rest, build it on [interval](/interval) as a flat sequence instead: work 20, rest 10, and set the rounds to cover every block, accepting that your long rest is a manual pause. Or set up something like [20/10 x 8](/tabata/20-10-8) and just hit start again.

Warm up for five minutes regardless. A protocol that begins at maximum in the first second does not forgive a cold start, and the four minutes you are saving by skipping it are not worth the fortnight you might spend on a calf.
