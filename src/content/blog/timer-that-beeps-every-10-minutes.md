A timer that beeps every 10 minutes keeps going after the first beep. That's the difference from a ten-minute countdown. You get a gentle cue every ten minutes, for an hour or three, and you can ignore it in between.

## Who uses one

People want this for all sorts of reasons, and they all need the same thing.

- **Study and work check-ins.** Not a full pomodoro, just a nudge every ten minutes. It's a quick chance to see if you're still on the task you meant to do.
- **Sauna and cold exposure.** Rounds you'd rather not time by feel, from a bench where you're not holding a phone.
- **Saline soaks and wound care.** Ten minutes on, remove, repeat. It's hard to judge ten minutes well while doing something else.
- **Contact lens solutions and cleaning steps.** Several steps where each one takes the same time.
- **Tattoo aftercare, masks, dye, and anything with a "leave on" step.** Same idea again.
- **Oven and kitchen checks.** Stirring a risotto, turning a tray, checking a slow roast. Not "is it done?" but "time to look again".
- **Long drives or long tasks.** Reminders to check your posture, drink some water or rest your eyes.

None of these need to be exact to the second. All of them need the beep to arrive on its own.

## Why phone timers struggle with this

The built-in timer on iPhone and Android runs one countdown. When it reaches zero, it rings and stops. To repeat, you have to restart it by hand every ten minutes. Or you set several alarms at clock times, which takes some maths.

Clock alarms are tied to the time of day, not to when you started. Say you set alarms for 10:00, 10:10, 10:20 and so on. If you begin two minutes late, every cue is two minutes off. If you finish early, they keep going off unless you turn each one off.

A repeating interval is simpler. You press start once, the cues are spaced from that moment, and there's a clear end.

## How to set it up

A repeating beep is just an interval workout with no rest.

1. Open the [beep every 10 minutes timer](/interval/beep-every-10-minutes).
2. Work is 10:00. Rest is **0**.
3. Rounds decide the total length. Six rounds is an hour.
4. Press Start.

With rest at zero, there's no gap between rounds. The timer runs straight through and beeps at each boundary. An EMOM works the same way underneath, so [EMOM workouts explained](/blog/emom-workouts-explained) describes the same idea with different labels.

To change the gap, change the work time. For five minutes, use [beep every 5 minutes](/interval/beep-every-5-minutes). For any other length, type it in on the [interval](/interval) timer.

## Picking the total

Rounds times the interval gives you the session length. It's better to set it a little long than a little short. A timer that ends early during a soak is more annoying than one extra beep.

| Every  | Rounds | Runs for  |
| ------ | ------ | --------- |
| 10 min | 3      | 30 min    |
| 10 min | 6      | 1 hour    |
| 10 min | 9      | 1.5 hours |
| 10 min | 12     | 2 hours   |
| 10 min | 18     | 3 hours   |

Not sure how long you'll need? Set lots of rounds and stop the timer when you're done. An unused round costs you nothing.

## What happens in a background tab

It's worth knowing how this works when the tab is hidden.

The timer doesn't count ticks. It remembers when you pressed Start and checks the clock each time it wakes. So if your laptop sleeps for forty minutes, it comes back showing the right time. That part is reliable.

The beep is a different story. Browsers slow down background tabs, and a phone with the screen off may pause the page. So a beep can come late or not at all. If the page wakes and finds that some beeps were missed, it plays one catch-up tone instead of a burst.

If you need to hear every beep, keep the page open and on screen. That goes for wound care or a sauna round. While the timer runs, it asks the device to keep the screen on, which works on most devices. For a loose study check-in, a background tab can do, as long as you glance at it. The time left shows in the tab title, so a glance tells you where you are.
