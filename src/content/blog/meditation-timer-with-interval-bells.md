A meditation timer with interval bells does one thing a plain countdown cannot: it marks the middle. Instead of a start, a long undifferentiated stretch and an end, you get a sit divided into parts, with a quiet cue at each boundary that asks nothing of you except that you notice it.

## Why a bell in the middle helps

Three reasons, and they are separate.

**Re-anchoring.** Twenty minutes into a sit you are usually somewhere else. Not dramatically — planning a conversation, rerunning an argument, composing an email. Left alone you may not notice for another ten minutes. A single tone interrupts that without judgement and puts you back at the breath. It is the same mechanism as noticing you have wandered, except the timer does the noticing for you when you have stopped doing it yourself.

**Phases.** Some practices are naturally sequential. Body scan then breath. Breath then open awareness. Loving-kindness moving outward from yourself to a stranger. Doing those by feel means checking a clock, which breaks the thing you are doing. A bell at the boundary means you can keep your eyes shut and still know where you are.

**Timing sitting and walking.** In many retreat settings sitting alternates with slow walking, and a bell is the usual signal for the change. Walking gets the blood back into the legs so that the next sit is about attention rather than about your knee. This is where interval bells stop being a nicety and become the schedule.

## Common structures

None of these are prescribed by anything. They are the shapes that get used because the arithmetic is easy and the segments are long enough to settle into.

| Total  | Bells    | Typical use                                 |
| ------ | -------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| 20 min | every 10 | Sit with one posture check in the middle    |
| 30 min | every 10 | Three phases, or three re-anchors           |
| 45 min | every 15 | Sit, walk, sit — or three thirds of one sit |
| 60 min | every 20 | Retreat length, with permission to move     |

For a worked example, [30 minutes with bells every 10](/meditation/30-minutes) is body scan, breath, open awareness, one phase per segment. [20 minutes with a bell at ten](/meditation/20-minutes) is the simpler version: one practice, one posture check.

The rule of thumb is that a segment shorter than about five minutes is not a phase, it is an interruption. If you want a nudge every two minutes you are looking for a different tool.

## What to do at the bell

Usually nothing. That is worth saying, because the instinct is to treat the sound as an instruction.

If you are running one practice throughout, the bell is a checkpoint. Notice where your attention actually is. Notice where your posture has drifted — shoulders up, chin forward, spine collapsed into a C. Let those come back. Then carry on. The whole thing takes a couple of breaths and does not need to become an event.

If you are running phases, the bell is the switch, and it helps to decide the sequence before you sit rather than choosing mid-session with your eyes closed.

If you are alternating sitting and walking, stand up when it sounds. Do not finish a thought first.

## Why one bell and not an alarm

An alarm is designed to be impossible to ignore, which is the opposite of what a sit needs. Repeating beeps, a rising three-note chime, a vibration pattern: all of those yank you out of a settled state and replace it with the small jolt of having been alerted. You then spend thirty seconds getting back to where you were, which defeats the point of a mid-sit cue.

A single soft tone with a long decay reaches you without startling you. It is also unambiguous in a way a pattern is not: one strike means one boundary, so you never have to decode whether a sound was a start, a phase change or the end. The [meditation timer](/meditation) here uses exactly one bell everywhere — one to begin, one at each interval, one to finish. No ticks, no rising-and-falling pairs, no countdown beeps in the last three seconds.

## How this timer does it

The bell is a generated tone rather than a recorded singing bowl, and it is the same tone every time. Before the first bell there is a ten second settle-in so you are not scrambling into position as the sit starts.

Timing is derived from timestamps, not counted ticks, so a phone that dims or a tab that gets backgrounded comes back with the correct elapsed time rather than however far behind it drifted. The screen stays awake while the page is in front, using a wake lock with a fallback for browsers that lack it. Turn the brightness down by hand before you start.

The honest limitation: the bell only plays while the page is open. The clock stays right through a lock, but the sound needs the tab in front, so leave it there. Mute and vibration are both toggles if you want one without the other, and the interval and total are editable in Settings. Everything is stored in the browser and there is no account.
