How many rounds in a boxing match depends entirely on the level: a professional world title fight is 12 rounds of three minutes, a small-hall six-rounder is six, and an amateur bout is three. The round length is the thing that stays mostly constant, and that is what your timer actually cares about.

## Professional boxing: 12 rounds

Championship distance was 15 rounds for most of the twentieth century. It became 12 after Duk-koo Kim collapsed following a 14th-round stoppage against Ray Mancini in November 1982 and died days later. The WBC moved to 12 rounds that same year; the WBA and IBF followed over the next few years, and by the late 1980s 12 was standard across the major bodies.

Non-title professional fights are matched to the fighters' experience, and the card works its way up:

| Fight type                   | Rounds | Round | Rest  | Total fight time |
| ---------------------------- | ------ | ----- | ----- | ---------------- |
| Debut / early prospect       | 4      | 3 min | 1 min | 15 min           |
| Developing pro               | 6      | 3 min | 1 min | 23 min           |
| Domestic level, eliminators  | 8      | 3 min | 1 min | 31 min           |
| Regional titles, main events | 10     | 3 min | 1 min | 39 min           |
| World titles                 | 12     | 3 min | 1 min | 47 min           |

Total time counts the rests between rounds but not the one after the final bell, which does not happen.

## Amateur and Olympic boxing: 3 rounds

Amateur bouts under World Boxing and the previous AIBA rules are three rounds of three minutes for elite men, with one minute between. That was not always the case: the format went to four two-minute rounds in 2000 and back to three threes in 2009 for elite men, so older sources disagree with each other. Youth and junior categories run shorter rounds, typically two minutes or less depending on age band.

## Women's boxing round length

Professional women's bouts have historically been **two-minute rounds**, usually ten of them for a title, which is 20 minutes of work against a men's 36.

This has been changing. In 2023 a handful of high-profile women's fights were contested over **three-minute rounds** after the fighters and promoters pushed for parity, and several sanctioning bodies have since allowed the longer format where both camps agree. It is still not the default, and it is negotiated fight by fight rather than mandated, so treat "women's boxing is two-minute rounds" as the usual case with a growing set of exceptions rather than a rule.

If you are training for a two-minute-round format, do not just train three-minute rounds and assume it transfers. The pacing is different and the work rate expected inside two minutes is higher.

## Setting the timer for each format

The default is 3 minutes work, 1 minute rest, and the [boxing round timer](/interval/boxing-rounds-3-1) is set that way out of the box. Change the round count to match what you are training for:

| Training for         | Work | Rest | Rounds |
| -------------------- | ---- | ---- | ------ |
| Amateur bout         | 3:00 | 1:00 | 3      |
| Women's pro (2-min)  | 2:00 | 1:00 | 10     |
| Women's pro (3-min)  | 3:00 | 1:00 | 10     |
| Four-round pro debut | 3:00 | 1:00 | 4      |
| Ten-round pro        | 3:00 | 1:00 | 10     |
| World title distance | 3:00 | 1:00 | 12     |

Two adjustments worth knowing about.

**Shorter rest for conditioning.** Cutting the rest to 30 seconds while keeping three-minute rounds is a standard way to make a session harder without adding rounds. It is not fight-realistic, which is the point.

**Longer rounds for pacing.** Some coaches run four-minute rounds so that three minutes feels short on the night. Fine occasionally, bad as a default, because your output over four minutes is not your output over three and you will train yourself into a pace that loses rounds.

For everything other than sparring, a plain [interval timer](/interval) with your own numbers does the job, and a [stopwatch](/stopwatch) is better for pad work where you want to time a specific drill rather than a round.

## Why audio matters more here than anywhere

You cannot look at a screen while your hands are moving. The whole value of a round timer is that the bell tells you and you never check. That means the beep at 2:30 and the beep at 3:00 have to be distinguishable from each other and audible over a heavy bag, which is a genuinely loud object.

Phone on a bench, volume up, and start it before you glove up rather than trying to tap a screen with tape on your hands. More on the specifics in [boxing round timer: 3-minute rounds](/blog/boxing-round-timer-3-minute-rounds).
