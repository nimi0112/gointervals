The pomodoro technique is 25 minutes of single-task work followed by a 5 minute break, repeated four times, then a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. Francesco Cirillo came up with it as a university student in the late 1980s, using a tomato-shaped kitchen timer, which is where the name comes from. Pomodoro is Italian for tomato.

## The mechanism is not the 25

People fixate on the number. The number is somewhat arbitrary and Cirillo has said as much; he started with what his kitchen timer could do and settled on a length that was short enough to commit to and long enough to finish something.

What actually does the work is the **commitment boundary**. A pomodoro is indivisible. If you break it to check a message, it does not count and you start a new one. That rule is the technique. The timer is just the enforcement mechanism.

The second mechanism is the **interruption log**. Cirillo's original method has you write down anything that comes up mid-pomodoro on a sheet, rather than acting on it. Most of what interrupts you turns out to be either unimportant or fine to handle 18 minutes later. Writing it down gets it out of working memory without breaking the block.

## When 25 is wrong

Twenty-five minutes is short for anything that needs a long spin-up. Writing, debugging, reading dense material, and design work all have a cost to re-entering context, and if that cost is five minutes then you are paying 20% overhead every block.

Useful adjustments:

| Task type                   | Work   | Break  | Why                         |
| --------------------------- | ------ | ------ | --------------------------- |
| Admin, email, small tasks   | 25 min | 5 min  | Standard. Low re-entry cost |
| Writing, coding, analysis   | 50 min | 10 min | One re-entry, not two       |
| Deep reading, hard problems | 90 min | 20 min | Matches an ultradian cycle  |
| Anything you are avoiding   | 10 min | 3 min  | The point is starting       |

The 90-minute version leans on ultradian rhythms, the roughly 90 to 120 minute cycles of alertness that Nathaniel Kleitman described. Treat that as a rough shape rather than a precise prescription. Most people notice a real dip somewhere past the hour mark.

The 10-minute version is the most underrated. If a task has been on your list for a week, the problem is starting, not sustaining. Ten minutes is small enough that the excuse collapses.

## The break is not optional and not your phone

Five minutes of scrolling is not a break. It is the same attentional load in a different posture, and you come back to the desk in worse shape than you left it.

Actual breaks: stand up, walk, get water, look out a window at something more than six metres away, stretch. Cirillo is specific that the break should be genuinely unrelated to work, because the point is letting the material consolidate rather than continuing to chew on it.

The long break after four pomodoros matters more than people think. Two hours of real focus is a lot, and pushing straight into a fifth block is where the quality drops off a cliff without you noticing.

## Realistic expectations

Most people who honestly track it manage **six to ten** pomodoros in a working day at the 25-minute length. Not sixteen. If you are planning your day around twelve solid blocks, you are planning a day that does not exist.

Start by estimating pomodoros per task before you begin, then record what it actually took. After two weeks you will have a calibration curve for your own estimates, which is quietly the most valuable output of the whole system.

## When it does not fit

The technique is bad for work that is genuinely reactive, like on-call, support, or anything where interruption is the job. It is also bad when you are already in flow: a timer going off mid-thought is a net loss. Cirillo's answer is that you finish the pomodoro and then keep going if you want; the timer marks the minimum, not the maximum.
