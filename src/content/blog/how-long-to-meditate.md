The question of how long to meditate gets answered with a lot of confidence by people who have not looked at what the evidence supports. The short version is that the length matters less than whether you sit again tomorrow, and that every commonly cited number is a convention rather than a finding.

## What the research roughly supports

Studies of daily practice in the range of about ten to fifteen minutes have reported measurable changes on attention and mood measures over a few weeks. That is a real body of work and it is also a messy one: sample sizes are often small, control conditions vary, self-reported outcomes are hard to blind, and the interventions differ enough that pooling them is questionable. Meta-analyses in this area tend to land on modest effects with wide uncertainty.

So the fair reading is that something in the ten to fifteen minute range, done most days, is enough to produce effects some studies can detect. Not that ten minutes is optimal, and not that fifteen beats twelve. Nobody has run the trial that would tell you that, and the dose-response curve for meditation is largely unmapped.

Worth saying plainly: this is not medical advice, and meditation is not a treatment for anything. If you are sitting because something is genuinely wrong, that is a conversation for a professional rather than a timer page.

## The twenty minute convention

Twenty minutes turns up constantly. Transcendental Meditation teaches twenty minutes twice a day as its standard, which is probably the single biggest reason the number is so familiar in the West. Twenty to twenty-five minutes is also a common sitting period in many Zen and Vipassana settings, though the specifics vary a lot between teachers and lineages, and some sit considerably longer.

None of those numbers came from measurement. Twenty seems to be roughly where a sit stops feeling brief and still stays physically comfortable without training. It is a good number for that reason and not because anything was tested against nineteen.

## Longer sits and retreats

Intensive retreat schedules commonly use forty-five or sixty minute sits, often alternating with periods of slow walking, and a full retreat day can add up to many hours. Those lengths exist inside a supported context: a teacher, a schedule, nothing else to do, and people around you doing the same thing.

Trying to jump straight to an hour alone in your bedroom usually fails for physical reasons rather than mental ones. An hour on a cushion is mostly a posture problem. Grow into it or borrow the structure — an hour with a bell every twenty is much more sittable than an unbroken hour.

## Consistency beats length

If you are going to optimise one variable, optimise the number of days you sit, not the minutes per day.

Five minutes every day is roughly thirty-five minutes a week, which is more than one ambitious forty-minute sit on a Sunday, and it builds the habit rather than the memory of having once done a thing. It also keeps the practice small enough that you never have to decide whether you have time, which is where most attempts die.

## How to progress

A sequence that works for most people:

1. **Five minutes, daily, for two weeks.** The goal is attendance, not quality. [Five minutes](/meditation/5-minutes) with a bell at each end.
2. **Ten minutes, daily, for a month.** This is where a sit starts having a shape: a couple of minutes of settling, then the actual practice. [Ten minutes](/meditation/10-minutes) is where most people can stop if they want to.
3. **Twenty minutes when ten feels short.** Not when ten feels easy — when you notice you are still going after the bell. [Twenty minutes with a bell at ten](/meditation/20-minutes) gives you a posture check halfway, which you will need.

Move up when the current length stops being the limiting factor. Move back down without ceremony when life gets busy; a shorter sit is a sit.

## When a timer helps and when it does not

A timer helps because it removes clock-watching. Without one, part of your attention stays on the question of how long it has been, and that part is the one you were trying to free up. It also stops you from quietly shortening every sit by two minutes, which is what happens when you finish by feel.

A timer hurts when the sit becomes about reaching the bell. If you find yourself enduring the last five minutes, or feeling that a sit that ended early does not count, the number has become the point. Sitting through restlessness is useful practice; sitting through a session you have stopped participating in is just waiting.

The other failure mode is escalation. Adding five minutes every week until it becomes unsustainable, then stopping entirely, is worse than having stayed at ten. Length is not a score.
